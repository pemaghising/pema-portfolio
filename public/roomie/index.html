Fair isn't equal.

Designing a household app that makes fairness visible, so nobody has to be the one who asks.

00 — The brief

Roommates keep things fair with memory, WhatsApp and a chart on the fridge. When schedules change, one person quietly takes on the extra work, and asking for help sounds like an accusation.

Role
Lead Graphic Designer (solo): research, strategy, IA, UI
Team
Me, with feedback from Leapfrog UX Bootcamp mentors
Timeline
2 weeks
Tools
Figma, FigJam, AI-assisted synthesis
Platform
iOS & Android · Kathmandu Valley

Problem

When schedules change, one person quietly absorbs the extra work, and asking for help sounds like an accusation.

Solution

Roomie weights chores by effort, keeps heavy tasks off your busy days, turns missed chores into a pool anyone can claim, and sends the reminder itself.

Impact

A focused V1 of 27 hi-fi screens covering onboarding, chores, pantry and settings. Money and AI were cut to Phase 2 on purpose. Not yet tested: the plan is below.

Most households already have a system.

It's usually a WhatsApp group, a verbal agreement and whoever notices the overflowing bin first. Roomie is for people who share a flat: students, young professionals and friends splitting rent in Kathmandu, Lalitpur and Bhaktapur.

Business goal

Freemium. Core coordination (chores, grocery list, reminders) is free forever. A household subscription, Roomie Plus, unlocks smarter automation later. Growth comes through invitations: every household that signs up brings two or three more users.

Constraints

  • Solo, two weeks. Research, strategy and UI were all mine.
  • Local context. NPR; eSewa, Khalti, Fonepay; WhatsApp as the default channel for everything.
  • No engineering partner. Every feature had to justify its build cost on paper first.
  • Public evidence first. Research built on competitor products and real app-store reviews. Personas are hypotheses to test.

Competitive landscape

The strongest competitor wasn't an app.

Four direct competitors, two indirect. The third ring was the real one: the habits. A group chat costs nothing and everyone already uses it.

  • Direct: Flatastic, Sweepy, Nipto, Tody
  • Indirect: Splitwise, Settle Up
  • Habits: WhatsApp, Google Keep, memory

What I found

  1. 01

    The category already exists.

    Flatastic already combines chores, shopping and expenses. "All-in-one" isn't a differentiator.

  2. 02

    Apps track; they don't manage.

    Most answer what, who, when. None answer why this is fair or what should change.

  3. 03

    Fair isn't equal.

    Taking out the trash takes two minutes. A bathroom deep-clean takes forty-five. Counting tasks treats them the same.

  4. 04

    The burden is thinking, not doing.

    Sweepy reviewers describe relief from not having to think about cleaning, not from cleaning less.

  5. 05

    Households aren't static.

    Tody reviewers ask for the plan to bend when they travel or get sick.

  6. 06

    Nobody wants a game imposed on them.

    Nipto reviewers want control over the rules.

  7. 07

    Money needs receipts.

    In Settle Up's reviews, trust comes from seeing the breakdown, not just the total.

→ Decision

Don't position Roomie as chores + expenses + groceries. Position it as a neutral third party that carries the mental and social load.

Four insights

  1. 01

    Fairness is effort, not count.

    A fair system has to weigh tasks.

  2. 02

    The real chore is remembering.

    Planning, assigning and following up is invisible work.

  3. 03

    A reminder from a friend reads as criticism.

    The 5 Whys ended at the same place every time: there is no neutral intermediary. Hypothesis · not yet validated

  4. 04

    Automation is trusted only when it's explainable.

    "Why me, why this week" has to be answerable.

Problem statement

Roommates struggle to manage shared chores and groceries fairly because they rely on memory, scattered chats and manual coordination. As schedules change, nobody is clearly responsible for what, which creates mental load, awkward conversations and repeated friction.

How might we

help roommates share a home in a way that feels fair, effortless, and free from awkward conversations?

  • …make chore distribution feel fair despite changing schedules?
  • …reduce the mental load of remembering, coordinating and following up?
  • …let the system do the reminding, so nobody has to?

What I killed, and why

Gamified leaderboard

Competing for points turns housemates into rivals, against the "don't rank people" signal in the research.

Chore bounties

Rent discounts for doing someone's chore put a price on favours. That's exactly the tension I wanted to remove.

In-app eSewa transfers

A huge legal and engineering lift. Once people know what they owe, they can scan a QR in the wallet they already use.

AI meal planner

It solves a problem nobody had. The pain was coordination, not cooking.

Financial features only earn trust with complete, explainable breakdowns. A half-built ledger costs more trust than it earns.

Key screens · insight → decision 01 / 08

60-second onboarding

Insight If an app needs constant input, it becomes another chore.

Five common chores are suggested and the basics come pre-selected; tap to keep or remove. Busy days on a single screen, a six-character code to invite. The progress bar keeps it honest.

Effort you can see

Insight Fair isn't equal.

Adding a chore asks for an effort level (Light, Medium or Heavy) before anything else. Every task card carries its chip, so the balance is visible at a glance.

Busy days

Insight Households aren't static.

One promise, in plain words: "We won't assign you heavy tasks on these days."

Missed by roommates → Claim

Insight Reminders between friends read as criticism.

A missed chore doesn't ping the person who missed it. It moves to a list anyone can claim. The list shows what's undone, not who dropped it.

Thanks, not rankings

Insight Nobody wants to be compared.

"Wonderful job. +1 day streak." Celebration moments thank people without comparing them. The sprout grows with the household's streak.

Pantry boundaries

Insight Grocery conflicts are about unspoken rules.

Shared, My items, Roommates. Personal items carry a rule: Do not touch or Ask first. A restock request becomes a claimable chore, so groceries plug into the chore system instead of building a second one.

Nudges people choose

Insight Nobody wants a game forced on them.

Each person picks a nudge style: Motivate me (streaks, emoji, warmth) or Just the facts (quiet, text only).

The app does the asking

Insight There is no neutral intermediary. So build one.

The lock screen does the reminding, in the voice of a flatmate who's on your side: "Gentle Nudge: The bathroom is waiting for a quick wipe down!"

Chapter five

Test

How would I know I'm right?

Honest status Roomie hasn't been tested with users yet. This is the plan, not a result.

Round 1 · moderated usability test

5–6 people living with roommates in the Kathmandu Valley, ideally two full households. 30 minutes each on the Figma prototype.

TaskPass signal
Set up a new home and invite a roommateDone in under 60s without help
Add a heavy chore and mark your busy daysCan explain why the chore won't land on those days
Claim a chore from "Missed by roommates"Understands it without guidance; doesn't read it as blame
Request a pantry restockExpects it to appear as a chore

The riskiest assumption

Do streaks and effort debt feel motivating, or shaming?

The research warns against ranking people, but V1 uses streaks and gives heavier loads to people who miss tasks. I'd show both nudge styles and the effort-debt state and ask one question:

"How would you feel if your roommate saw this?"

Decision rule If more than 1 in 5 participants says "judged", streaks become opt-in and effort debt becomes private.

Round 2 · concierge pilot

Before building anything: run the Sunday chore plan by hand for two households over WhatsApp for two weeks. That tests whether a neutral planner actually reduces friction, the core bet, before the code does.

06 — Reflect

Fair isn't equal changed how I design.

My first instinct as a visual designer was to make chores look nice. The real work was defining what fair means and making it visible: effort chips, busy days, and a pool with no names attached.

Cutting my top-scoring feature was the most product-minded thing I did.

RICE said build expenses first. Context said the money half is already solved and needs a trust bar I couldn't reach in two weeks. The scores informed the decision; they didn't make it.

Real roommates, sooner.

Everything I know about Roomie comes from public reviews, competitor products and structured reasoning. It's real evidence, but it isn't the people who'll use the app. Next time I'd put the concept in front of real roommates before hi-fi, not after.

Inconsistencies I'd have caught sooner

  • The profile screen shows a household rank, which cuts against the no-ranking principle.
  • The roommates screen lists "Mumbai" in a Kathmandu product.
  • The Home screen's nav bar highlights Chores instead of Home.
